# Three finished posts, with the image brief for each

**These are our own posts and our own numbers.** They are here to show the shape,
not to be copied. A client's post carries the client's facts: lifting the numbers
below into someone else's feed is exactly the failure this kit exists to prevent.

Each example has three parts: the post as it goes out, what makes it work, and the
image brief - enough for an agent to build the picture without asking.

All three are written in paragraphs, because that is how their author writes. That
is a voice, not the rule: another author ships short broken lines with ❌/✅ markers,
and their posts should keep that shape. Take the shape from the client's `voice.md`,
the craft from here.

---

## 1. Measurement

```
We sent the same offer two ways to the same kind of people. One got 11% replies,
the other 3.4%.

Same list and the same sender, in the same week. The only difference was the shape
of the first message.

The 3.4% version did what everyone does. It opened with who we are, listed what
the product does in bullets, and ended with a call booking link.

The 11% version was under 340 characters. One line on why this person
specifically, one sentence on the single thing we do that is relevant to them, and
a question that can be answered with one word.

The mechanism is boring: a bulleted list reads as a broadcast, so it is answered
like one. A question that costs four seconds to answer gets answered, and the
second message is where the actual conversation starts.

The part I did not expect: messages four and five in the same sequence got zero
replies, across the whole run. After three touches a cold thread is closed, and the
follow-up only annoys the people who already decided.

If you are running outreach right now, open your sequence and count the characters
in message one. Over 600 and you are writing a brochure.
```

**Why it works.** The first line takes a side and carries a number - the reader
either recognises their own numbers or wants to argue. The mechanism is named, not
just the result. The inside fact is the zero on messages four and five, which only
someone who ran it would know, and admitting it costs us something. The action is
specific and takes one minute.

**Image brief.** Number layout. Figure: `11% vs 3.4%`. Kicker: `Measured, 3,812
invites`. Line beneath: "same list, same sender - the difference was the shape of
the first message". Accent colour on the figure, nothing else on the card.

## 2. Position

```
Where the list came from mattered more than what we wrote in it. 29.3% replies
against 6.8%, same message.

Hooks, frameworks, the perfect opener: the copy is what gets sold. We spent a
year on that too, and the copy is worth about three points. The list is worth
twenty.

Two sources, same sequence, same week. One list was built from a described
portrait, with every profile actually read before it went in. The other was a
decent export with sensible filters - the kind most teams send to and consider
fine.

The mechanism is not mysterious. A person who matches the portrait recognises the
first sentence as being about them, so they read the second. A person who merely
passes a filter reads a message addressed to someone else, and the best opener
ever written does not fix being the wrong person.

What this costs to admit: it means most of the work happens before anyone writes
anything, and it is boring work. Reading profiles is boring. It is also the
difference between one reply in three and one in fifteen.

If your reply rate is under 10%, do not rewrite the message this week. Take twenty
rows of the list and check who is actually in it.
```

**Why it works.** The first line is a position with a number that contradicts what
the niche sells, so the reader either argues or keeps reading. The mechanism is
named in one plain paragraph. The admission that the real work is boring is the
part that reads as honest. The action redirects them away from the thing they were
about to do.

**Image brief.** Number layout. Figure: `29.3% / 6.8%`. Kicker: `Same message, two
lists`. Line beneath: "where the list came from, measured across 3,812 invites".

## 3. Advice, with a checklist

```
A bad list costs more than a bad message, and you can see it before you send
anything.

Four checks, ten minutes, on a sample of twenty rows. Twenty rows tell you what
the whole file would.

Open twenty profiles at random and ask:

Is this the country you asked for, or just a company with an office there
Is this a real current role, or a title from four years ago nobody updated
Would this person recognise the problem you are about to name, in one sentence
Is the company the size you asked for, or did a dictionary word pull in a
different company with a similar name

Anything under about seventy percent on those four and the list is not ready. Send
to it anyway and you will spend the next two weeks reading replies that say "wrong
person", and you will blame the message.

The one that catches people out is the last. A company whose name is also an
ordinary word quietly brings in a dozen others with the same word in their name.
On one run that was thirteen of twenty five rows, and the cost per good lead
doubled - from a filter that looked fine.

Take twenty rows out of your current list and run the four checks before your next
send.
```

**Why it works.** It is advice with a cost attached, which is what separates it
from a tips post. The inside fact is the thirteen-of-twenty-five, with the
consequence in money. The action is small enough to actually be done.

**Image brief.** Checklist layout, four items, exactly as the four questions in the
post but shortened to one line each. Kicker: `Before you send`. Headline: `Four
checks on twenty rows`.

## How to use these with a client

1. Read the three with them and ask which one they could have written. The answer
   tells you which type to start with.
2. Find the equivalent fact in their business - the number they measured, the thing
   they changed their mind about, the mistake they keep seeing.
3. If no such fact exists yet, you are not ready to write: go back to `mining`.
   Starting without one produces the exact content they are paying to avoid.
