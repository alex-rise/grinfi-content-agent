# The craft: writing a post people read

The rules are universal. The numbers beside them are what they were measured on:
our own publishing, a full read of profiles with unusually strong response, and
four large channels taken apart end to end. Where a number belongs to one
business it is marked **measured** - a new client will have their own.

## 0. The test that catches empty text

Put another company's name into the finished post. **If the text survives, there
was nothing to say: delete the whole thing.** A good post falls apart under that
substitution, because it rests on something the others do not have.

Then find the sentence that anyone could have written about anything, and cut it.
It is usually the first or the last.

## 1. The first line

It decides whether the rest is read: the feed truncates a post after two or three
lines.

One of three forms works:

- **A claim that takes a side.** "Looking at a profile before you invite buys you
  nothing." The reader either agrees or argues; both read on.
- **"I" plus a concrete result.** "Closed a 25k deal after ten minutes of reading
  the client." _Measured: posts with "I" in the first line got roughly double the
  response of the same author's other posts._
- **A fork with no ready answer.** "Who do you write to first inside a company?"
  A question the reader cannot answer and you can.

Do not start with: a rhetorical question ("do you know what X and Y have in
common?" - _measured: two such posts were the worst in a sample of fifty_), a
bare statistic, a quotation, a greeting, or an announcement.

A number that takes a side is a claim and belongs to the first form. A number
that only reports ("buyers now need more touchpoints than before") is the bare
statistic, and it reads like the first slide of somebody else's deck.

**The first line must break off.** The thought does not finish there, or there is
no reason to expand the post. The mechanism: a short first line collapses a long
post into "see more". The click alone is not the win: what is reported to count
now is whether the reader stays after it (dwell time), and a reader who expands
and leaves within seconds is worth less than one who never clicked. So a short,
unfinished line beats a complete thought in two lines, and the first lines under
the fold have to pay it off.

## 2. The body: three required elements

In any order, but all three.

1. **A mechanism, not advice.** Not "profile views do not work", but "the view
   notification only reaches paid accounts, which are a minority, and the volume
   of views is what the platform treats as grounds for a limit". Advice without a
   mechanism reads as opinion; a mechanism is remembered.
2. **An inside fact that cannot be invented.** What happened on a demo, what the
   platform did to a colleague's account, "the list had 4,998 rows and 437 went
   into work". The boring specifics - the name, the sum, the date, the number -
   are exactly the parts that cannot be faked.
3. **One action at the end.** Check it against your own numbers, recount something
   in your own spreadsheet, a real question. Not "what do you think?".

No inside fact at all means the post does not go out. This is the main governor
of the whole kit: nothing to say means do not write.

**A number from outside needs its definition, not only its source.** An industry
benchmark can support a post, but `facts.md` holds what it actually measures.
Before: a published count of touchpoints per deal was quoted as the touches a
seller makes; checked later, it counted every ad click and email open across a
sample of well-funded SaaS companies. After: quote the number with what it
counts, or leave it out.

## 3. The ending

- **A real question**, one that cannot be answered yes or no, or the comments
  will be emoji. Platforms value comments above likes, and you answer **every
  one** - it pulls people back into the post and earns a second wave of reach.
  _Measured: where the ask was generic ("share in the comments"), the median was
  three comments._
- Or **an action with a number in it**: open your CRM and count how many leads
  are marked "not interested" without ever having said no.
- A link goes in the first comment rather than the text. That is the default,
  not a law: reports from 2026 disagree on how much a link in the body costs, so
  test it on your own numbers before arguing about it.
- **A one-line P.S.** - the nearest event, the course, the recording. The
  postscript sells; the body does not.

## 4. Layout

- **The shape of the text comes from `voice.md`, not from this file.** Some
  people write in paragraphs. Others write in short broken lines, a break after
  almost every sentence, with emoji as list markers. Both are fine when they are
  that person's habit. Before: a voice that ships short lines and ❌/✅ lists was
  rewritten into tidy paragraphs, and the approver read the paragraphs as machine
  text. After: copy the line rhythm of their approved posts.
- With no voice file yet: one thought per line, a blank line between paragraphs.
- **Emoji carry a meaning or are absent.** If the person uses them, `voice.md`
  holds a small dictionary - one emoji, one meaning (the mistake, the fix, the
  number) - and a cap per post. An emoji that means nothing is decoration, and
  decoration reads as a template.
- 900-1,800 characters on a long platform, 300-600 on a short one. _Measured:
  length alone decides nothing - a short post is not punished if it has an "I"
  and a fact._
- Steps as a numbered list. A "this, not that" contrast as a pair of lines.
- An image every time: response was lower without one across every profile read.
- No hashtags. Hashtag search does not pay for what it does to the text.

## 5. Five post types, so the feed does not become one formula

| Type | What is inside | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Measurement** | a number and what follows from it | a fresh measurement exists |
| **Case** | before → what we did → after | a project or task closed |
| **Tool** | what it gives and how to set it up | a new capability appeared |
| **Advice** | what to do, a checklist, a mistake | a question clients keep asking |
| **Position** | a claim with an argument | you disagree with the niche |

Plus three forms that work harder than the rest, and so are used no more than
once every few posts:

- **Debunking the advice of your own niche.** "Half the consultants sell this as
  step one" - then the mechanism for why it does not work. _Measured: the single
  best post on one profile in nine months._
- **A personal story with a lesson.** Being fired, a failure, the first client,
  "what eighteen years taught me". _Measured: the three best-performing posts on
  a profile with fifteen thousand followers were all this._
- **A numbers report, as a monthly series.** Revenue, what broke, what is not
  selling. The honesty is the technique: "we learned to sell the first
  subscription, the second one not yet".

## 6. The artefact-for-a-comment post

Once a week at most. A real artefact, whole - a spreadsheet, a checklist, a
teardown, a template - given away for a code word in the comments, sent by DM.

_Measured: these collected 80 to 410 comments against 3 to 13 on the same
author's ordinary posts._ It works even when the file is already public: people
pay a comment to avoid searching.

The conditions without which it only annoys: the artefact is genuinely useful on
its own, the code word is short, and the DMs go out the same day.

## 7. The stop list

Banned outright:

- **Long dashes.** A human being does not type them into a post field; it is the
  first marker of machine text. Short hyphens only.
- **Mirror pairs, the whole family**: "not just X, but Y", "this is not about
  X, it is about Y", "it's not X. It's Y.", "that's not X, that's Y", "not
  because X. Because Y.", "do X. Not Y." as a punchline, "nothing wrong with X.
  Y." After the long dash this symmetry is the most recognisable machine marker.
  Before delivering, search the draft for lines starting with "Not", "It's not",
  "That's not" or containing "isn't", and rewrite each one as two plain
  statements: not "It is not the copy. It is the list." but "The copy has little
  to do with it. The list decided it."
- Perfect triples: "fast, cheap, reliable". The rhythm of an advert, not of speech.
- "Let's dive in", "in today's fast-paced world", "spoiler", "and here is where
  it gets interesting", "here's the thing", "here's what nobody tells you", "the
  problem nobody mentions", "picture this", "imagine a...", "everyone says X,
  but...". The escalators too: "it gets worse", "here's the kicker".
- A closing moral paragraph that retells what was just said, or a truism ("at
  the end of the day", "the human touch").
- "What do you think?" glued to the end.
- A one-word paragraph for drama - unless standalone punchline lines are a
  recorded habit in `voice.md`, in which case they are voice and they stay.
- Decorative emoji in place of list bullets (meaningful markers from `voice.md`
  are allowed); more than one exclamation mark.
- Curly quotes. People type straight ones into a post field.

Vocabulary: delve, leverage, unlock, seamless, robust, game-changer, ecosystem,
synergy, transformation, powerful tool, revolution, navigate, foster, landscape,
realm, "plays a crucial role", "excited to share", "it is important to note",
"it is important to understand that". In any other language the principle is the same:
the words that appear in every vendor's post are the words to cut.

## 8. What makes text alive

- **Specifics instead of categories.** Not "a client", but "a twelve-person
  agency". Not "recently", but "on Tuesday". Not "grew", but "from 15 to 589".
- **An uneven rhythm.** A short sentence next to a long one. Machines even out
  sentence length; people do not.
- **Admitting uncertainty where it exists.** "I do not know whether it repeats."
  "That is one month of data, I could be wrong." Refusing to give a number you do
  not have is stronger than any number.
- You may start on a conjunction, leave a sentence incomplete, repeat a word.
- One thought per post. Three thoughts are three posts.
- Read it out loud. If it sounds like a report, rewrite it.

## 9. Where topics come from

- **From questions in DMs and comments**, never from a poll asking what to write
  about. Five similar questions in one day means thousands are thinking it.
- **From your own recordings**: webinars, calls, consultations. That is where the
  things you have already explained in your own words are kept.
- **From what you are doing right now.** Do interesting work first and there will
  be something to say, not the other way round.

## 10. How the writer gets better

Once a month, read your own best ten percent from the last ninety days. Look for
**one or two traits shared by all of the best ones** and carry them into every
next post. Do not analyse the bottom eighty percent: you learn from your own top,
not from your own average.

The audience decides quality, not the author. A post that feels weak is not cut
from the plan - it is published and measured.

## 11. Before saying a draft is ready

Run the whole list on every draft, and fix before showing it rather than after
the person points at it. A draft is not ready if any of these is true:

- It survives the company-name swap from section 0.
- It has no inside fact, or a number in it is missing from that client's
  `facts.md`.
- It contains a mirror pair, a banned opener or escalator, a stop-list word, a
  long dash or a curly quote.
- Its line shape does not match `voice.md` - paragraphs where they write short
  lines, or the other way round.
- It has the same structure as the previous post on the same channel.
- It carries more than one thought.
- It ends on a neat moral that retells the post.
- It cannot be read aloud by the person whose name is on it.
