# Planning: a week at a time, from material you already have

A content plan is not a list of topics. A list of topics is how you end up writing
general posts on a schedule: the slot exists, so something must fill it, and what
fills it is whatever a model can produce without facts.

**The plan is built from cores.** One core, one post. No core, no slot. If the week
has four slots and three cores, the week has three posts - and the fourth slot is a
signal to go back to `mining`, not a licence to invent.

## The working file

`business/clients/<client>/plan.md`. The six memory files hold what is true about
the business; this one is a queue and changes every week.

| Date | Channel | Type | Core | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 13.10 Mon | LinkedIn | measurement | C3 call volume | queued |
| 15.10 Wed | LinkedIn | position | C7 three months | waiting for approval |
| 17.10 Fri | Threads | advice | C4 library swap | draft |

Statuses, in order: `draft` → `waiting for approval` → `queued` → `published` →
`measured`. Nothing skips a step, and `queued` is only ever written after a human
said go.

## The week

- **Monday - material.** Read what arrived: new recordings, questions from the DMs
  and comments, anything the client dropped in the inbox folder. Mine what is new,
  then choose the week's cores and write them into `plan.md`.
- **Tuesday - writing.** All of the week's texts in one sitting, plus the images.
  Writing three posts together is faster than three separate days and keeps the
  week from repeating itself.
- **Tuesday evening - one approval batch.** Everything goes to the person in one
  message, not dripped through the day. Dripping is what makes clients stop
  reading.
- **Wednesday - queue.** What came back approved goes into the scheduler
  (`publishing`). What came back with edits is fixed and sent again the same day.
- **Every publishing day - the first hour.** Answer every comment. This is work,
  not waiting, and it is where half the reach comes from.
- **Friday - the short review.** What went out, what landed, what is missing for
  next week. Three lines, not a report.

## How much

- **Three posts a week per channel to start**, on one main channel. Not five, not
  daily.
- A second channel is added only when the first one has run four weeks without the
  approval queue backing up.
- The brake is almost never the writing. It is approval and material. Plan to the
  slowest of the three.

## The mix

Across any four posts, the types should differ. A month of positions reads as
someone with opinions and no work; a month of cases reads as a brochure.

- No two posts of the same type in a row on one channel.
- At least one **case** or **measurement** every two weeks - these are what make
  the rest credible.
- At most one **artefact-for-a-comment** post a week, and not in the first month.
- The **personal story** is the strongest single form and the easiest to overuse:
  once a month, not once a week.

## The calendar the business already has

Before inventing occasions, take the dates that exist: platform deadlines that
affect their clients, their own launches and events, the season their buyers plan
in, conferences they are going to, regulatory dates in their industry.

A dated post outperforms an undated one on the same subject, because it answers
"why am I reading this today". If the business has a date calendar - and most have
one they have never written down - that is the spine of the quarter.

## The first month, honestly

Weeks one and two are mostly collection: the interview, the recordings, the first
mining. Publish two posts a week in that period, not three, and say so in advance.
A client who was promised five a week and gets two feels failed; a client who was
told two and gets three feels looked after.

Do not open with the strongest material. The first posts are read by almost
nobody - the audience has to learn that this account publishes. Keep the best core
for week three or four, when there is someone to read it.

## When there is nothing to say

This happens, and it is not a crisis. In order:

1. **Go back to the recordings.** A thirty-minute call usually holds eight to
   twelve cores; most teams use three and forget the rest.
2. **Read the questions.** Five similar questions in the DMs this month is a post,
   and it writes itself.
3. **Publish the numbers.** What changed in the business this month, including what
   got worse. Nobody else does this, which is exactly why it works.
4. **Ask for one recording.** Thirty minutes buys a month.

What you never do: fill the slot with a general post to keep the schedule. One
empty week costs nothing. Four generic posts teach the audience to scroll past the
name, and that is expensive to undo.

## Monthly

- Read the top ten percent of the last ninety days and find the one or two traits
  all of them share. Those become the default for next month.
- Write three lines to the client: what got the most response and why you think so,
  what got the least, and what you are changing. Not a dashboard.
- Check the mix: if one type is missing entirely, the next month is skewed and it
  is usually because that type needs material nobody collected.
- Re-read `facts.md` and the red list. Businesses change what they are willing to
  say, usually without telling anyone.
